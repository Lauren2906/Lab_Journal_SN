1 Introduction

American youth is more likely than any other age-group to be a ‘spectator’ in social life, meaning that they discuss socially important topics privately, but are not necessarily active in their community (Shearer et al. 2026). Thus, while youth is discussing social topics, they are not acting on them in the same way that older generations are. This points to an interesting development in civil engagement; from engagement as a behavioural characteristic, to engagement as a social characteristic. Civic engagement as a social characteristic encompasses not just the behavioural side of engagement, but also the attitudes that people hold. This makes it more interesting to look at processes of civic attitude formation in youth, rather than focusing on civic behaviour.

Civic engagement is also found to be related to personal and societal benefits, such as increased trust (Schulz 2024), political participation (Lee 2022; Cohen and Chaffee 2013), and mental health (Fenn et al. 2024). Due to its benefits, civic engagement is sometimes viewed as an essential part of having a functioning democratic society (Crockett et al. 2023). It is thus essential to understand how and why people become civilly engaged, espescially now that the shape of civic engagement is changing. As youth is more likely to discuss social topics with their peers, social networks could be an interesting site for the spread of civic attitudes. Attitudes are found to be influenced by our social networks (Visser and Mirabile 2004; Konstantinou et al. 2021), suggesting that this could also be the case for civic attitudes.

There is some evidence that youth tend to have similar civic attitudes as their social context (Karkdijk et al. 2026). This points towards a degree of clustering within social networks, with regards to civic attitudes, where certain groups have more civilly engaged attitudes than others. However, Karkdijk et al. (2026) do not take a social network perspective in establishing this relationship, meaning that potentially important aspects of civic attitude formation are overlooked.

When taking an explicit social network perspective, civic engagement is predominantly viewed through a behavioural lens (Wegemer 2021; Oosterhoff et al. 2021). Wegemer (2021) finds that social networks do not affect an individual’s degree of activism. This, however, could be explained by youths inclination towards being spectators, rather than participators. Youth does not want to participate, regardless of whether their friends do. However, this relationship may be different for attitudes. Even if youth does not seems affected by their peers in their behaviour, their opinions may still change. Oosterhoff et al. (2021) look at the opposite effect, examining whether civic behaviour and attitudes affect social connectedness. They find that civic efficacy in youth is associated with having more social ties, again pointing at potential clustering in social networks, with regards to civic attitudes.

Thus, there seems to be some evidence that there may be clustering of civic attitudes in a network. This paper will look into potential segregation in networks with regards to civic attitudes, by examining first-year university students’ networks. Where previous social network research has focussed on early adolescents (Karkdijk et al. 2026; Wegemer 2021; Oosterhoff et al. 2021), this paper shifts towards older youth. This shift could give a more accurate reflection of attitudes in an environment where civic engagement is less directly stimulated than in high school, better reflecting real life situations. The first step in this paper is to look into clustering within students’ networks with regards to civic attitudes by answering the following question:

RQ1: Are students who are connected to each other more similar with regards to civic attitudes, than students who are not connected to each other?


Secondly, this paper will look into explanations for potential clustering of civic attitudes in social networks. Even if there is relatively little clustering in a network, it is possible that these behavioural acts are occurring within a network. Due to the complicated and interdependent nature of social networks, behavioural preferences may not directly lead to clusters on a macro-scale, as they are overshadowed by other processes, or cancel each other out.

This paper will look into two behavioural acts which may shape networks with regards to civic attitudes. Firstly, networks are shaped by individual preferences, where people prefer to form ties with others’ with similar attitudes (McPherson et al. 2001). If this is the case, civic attitudes are unlikely to spread throughout a social network, as people mostly interact with similar others. Secondly, network structures may be dependent on influence effects, where people’s attitudes become more similar to their friends’ (Marsden and Friedkin 1993). In this case, positive civic attitudes are able to spread throughout a network, potentially increasing the degree of positive civic engagement within a whole network.

Whether the influence effect actually takes place, is an open question. Civic socialisation seems to take place through the social connections that an individual has (Karkdijk et al. 2026), pointing towards an influence effect. However, this relation may be more complicated than people’s attitudes being similar to the attitudes of their classmates, as our social networks have the ability to shape our attitudes in complex ways. We do not simply copy the attitudes of our friends, as our friends may also change because of us and because of each other. Some of our classmates may also exert a greater influence on our attitudes, perhaps because we are connected to them, or because we want to be liked by them. In any case, a social network perspective is necessary to understand how and why individuals change their civic attitudes. This leads to the following research question:

RQ2: To what extent do students become more similar to their peers with regards to civic attitudes when they are connected in a network? And to what extent do students prefer to become connected with other students with similar civic attitudes?

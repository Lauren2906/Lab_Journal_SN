1 Introduction

American youth is more likely than any other age-group to be a ‘spectator’ in social life, meaning that they discuss socially important topics privately, but are not necessarily active in their community (Shearer et al. 2026). Thus, while youth is discussing social topics, they are not acting on them in the same way that older generations are. This points to an interesting development in civil engagement; from engagement as a behavioural characteristic, to engagement as a social characteristic.

Youth discuss social issues more than ever (SOURCE?), and the more a topic is discussed within a friend group, the more likely it is that the peers within the group take over the dominant opinion (SOURCE??????????). Increased discussion about civic topics, would thus suggest that individuals in a friendship network are more similar to their direct peers than to other individuals in their network in their civic engagement attitudes. This clustering of attitudes in a network could point at increasing segregation in civic attitudes, despite the positive personal and societal benefits to being civilly engaged. Engagement is found to be related to trust (XXX), voting (XXX) and XXX (XXX), aspects that could cluster in networks, making them more accessible to certain people than to others.

Civic engagement is a well-studied topic, also within social network analyses. One example is Wegemer (2021) who looks at civic behaviour in teens. He finds that social networks do not affect an individual’s degree of activism. This, however, could be explained by youths inclination towards being spectators, rather than participators. Youth does not want to participate, regardless of whether their friends do. However, this relationship may be different for attitudes. Even if youth does not seems affected by their peers in their behaviour, their opinions may still change.

This paper will look into civic engagement attitudes, rather than actual behaviour. By looking at slightly older youth than previous research, this paper will be able to give a more accurate reflection of attitudes in an environment where civic engagement is less directly stimulated than in high school, better reflecting real life situations. Thus, in this paper I will firstly answer the following question:

RQ1: Are students who are connected to each other more similar with regards to civic engagement attitudes, than students who are not connected to each other?


Clustering within social networks can have multiple reasons, thus to fully understand potential clustering, it is important to take into account why there is clustering. Clustering with regards to civil engagemetn attitudes within a social network could be due to preferences or due to social influence (SOURCE). Attitudes towards civic engagement may not be the first reason that people decide to become friends with one another, rather these processes may rely on attitudes and behaviour that is seen as more important to someone’s identity (SOURCE?). This leaves open the opportunity for social influences to take place, as people with different civic engagement attitudes can make ties together, allowing for civic attitudes to potentially spread through a network, rather than remaining within clusters of highly engaged individuals. This leads to the following research question:

RQ2: Do students become more similar to each other with regards to civic engagement, when they are connected in a network?
